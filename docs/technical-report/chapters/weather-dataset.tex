\section{Weather Dataset}
Our second source of data is hourly NYC weather, fetched live from the Open-Meteo archive API\footnote{\url{https://open-meteo.com/}}.

\subsection{Dataset Structure}

The weather dataset captures hourly atmospheric conditions across New York City and serves as a proxy for environmental factors that influence cycling demand. Each record represents a one-hour observation aligned to Eastern Time (America/New\_York), ensuring consistency with trip timestamps across the analysis.

\subsubsection{Original Structure}

The dataset from the Open-Meteo API includes the following core fields:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Timestamp}: the hourly observation time, localized to America/New\_York timezone.
  \item \textbf{Temperature (temperature\_2m)}: ambient air temperature at 2 metres above ground level, measured in degrees Celsius.
  \item \textbf{Precipitation}: the cumulative precipitation during the hour, measured in millimetres. Most hours report zero; nonzero values are sparse.
  \item \textbf{Weather Code}: the WMO weather code, a standardized numeric classification that encodes condition types (clear, cloudy, rain, snow, etc.).
  \item \textbf{Wind Speed (wind\_speed\_10m)}: instantaneous wind speed at 10 metres above ground level, measured in kilometres per hour.
  \item \textbf{Year}: the year of the observation, used for partitioning the dataset into annual Parquet files.
\end{itemize}

Unlike the City Bike dataset, where extracted features such as trip duration categories are derived during preprocessing, the weather dataset is used largely in its raw form. The year column is the only structural addition, used for file partitioning rather than analysis. If additional discretized weather categories emerge during exploration (e.g., temperature bins for ``cold,'' ``comfortable,'' ``hot''), they will be noted in the sections below.

Data are fetched from the Open-Meteo ``archive'' endpoint for the calendar year covering the ride data. The API request uses the NYC coordinates (\texttt{lat}: $40.7823234$, \texttt{lon}: $-73.9654161$), requests hourly \texttt{fields\ temperature\_2m}, \texttt{precipitation}, \texttt{weather\_code} and \texttt{wind\_speed\_10m}, and sets \texttt{timezone} to America/New\_York and \texttt{wind\_speed\_unit} kmh. The returned hourly time series is parsed into a dataframe with a datetime column and inspected for completeness (hours expected vs present).

The exploratory analysis of the weather dataset is organised around two core
questions: how weather variables are distributed and seasonal over time, and how
they relate to ridership. The first perspective summarises hourly temperature,
precipitation, wind, and the WMO weather codes using distributions and
seasonality plots. Temperature exhibits a clear annual cycle with daily and
seasonal variation; precipitation is highly skewed with most hours reporting
zero and occasional spikes that drive short-term drops in ridership. Wind shows
moderate variation and can amplify the perceived discomfort at low temperatures.

The second perspective connects weather to demand. Simple aggregated plots
relating average hourly ridership to binned temperature reveal the expected
pattern: ridership increases with temperature up to a comfortable range and
declines in cold conditions. Precipitation hours show a consistent reduction in
ridership, with heavier hourly totals producing larger drops. Weather codes can
be used as categorical annotations (clear, rain, snow) to summarise combined
effects: rainy or snowy hours have lower ridership even at similar temperatures.

Operationally, hourly ridership is computed by flooring trip start times to the hour and counting rides per hour. This rides-per-hour series is inner-joined to the weather hourly dataframe on the matching datetime; temperature is grouped into 5°C bins (e.g. 0–4°C, 5–9°C) to compute mean rides per bin and visualise the non-linear relation between temperature and demand.

These analyses justify including the weather dataset: it provides explanatory
variables that help account for systematic variation in cycling demand and
improves the interpretability of temporal ridership patterns.

\subsection{Data Quality}

Overall the archived hourly feed is high quality: timestamps are complete and
consistently aligned to America/New\_York, and the core variables are recorded
at regular hourly intervals. Important caveats remain. The feed effectively
represents a single observation point (Central Park or the archive's chosen
station), so it may not capture microclimate variation across the five boroughs.
This spatial simplification can introduce bias when local weather differs from
the station's measurements, but it is a pragmatic choice for citywide analysis.

Precipitation is sparse and right-skewed: many hours report zero and a few
hours contain most of the cumulative rainfall. When plotting histograms or
computing summary statistics, non-zero precipitation should be examined
separately to avoid misleading central tendencies. Temperature and wind are
continuous and well-behaved, but gusts or short anomalies can appear and should
be checked against outlier thresholds if they drive unusual model behaviour.

Practical notes from the exploration: histograms show temperature and wind distributions across the year; precipitation plots focus on non-zero hours to avoid the mass at zero. Mean monthly temperature plots clearly show the annual cycle. When joining to rides the notebook confirms a rise in mean hourly ridership with temperature up to a comfortable band and a drop in cold hours; rainy or snowy weather corresponds to lower ridership at similar temperatures.

Finally, the year column is an engineered field used for partitioning Parquet
files and does not affect analysis directly. If derived categorical features
(e.g. temperature bins or precipitation intensity classes) are created they
should be documented and justified in the analysis methods.
