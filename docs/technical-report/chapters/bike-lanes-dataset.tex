\section{Bike Lanes Dataset}
Our third source of data is NYC's bike-route network, published by NYC OpenData\footnote{\url{https://data.cityofnewyork.us/d/mzxg-pwib}} as dataset \texttt{mzxg-pwib}.
It is used to render bike infrastructure (lanes, paths, and their protection level) in the visualization.

\subsection{Dataset Structure}
The bike-lanes dataset describes New York City's bicycle infrastructure at the segment level. Unlike
the trip dataset, where each row is an individual ride, here each row corresponds to a physical piece
of the network: a lane, path, or related facility drawn as a line on the map. This makes the dataset
especially useful for spatial analysis, because the raw table is already close to its visual form. The
core fields tell us where a segment is, what kind of facility it is, whether it is still active, and
when it was installed or retired.

From the perspective of the final visualization, this dataset provides the infrastructure layer. It is
the source of the map geometry, but also of the attributes that control how each segment is classified
and styled. A protected route segment should be interpreted differently from a shared lane, and a
current segment should be treated differently from a retired one. For that reason, the dataset is not
just a geographic background: it is an analytical object that lets us talk about the structure and
evolution of the bike network itself.

\subsubsection{Original Structure}
The original CSV contains one row per route segment and includes the following relevant attributes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Segment ID}: the identifier of the route segment within the source dataset.
  \item \textbf{Bike ID}: a bike-network identifier used by the source to track the same segment
  across revisions.
  \item \textbf{Status}: whether the segment is currently active or has been retired from service.
  \item \textbf{Installation Date}: the date on which the segment was added to the network.
  \item \textbf{Retirement Date}: the date on which the segment was removed or replaced, when
  available.
  \item \textbf{the\_geom}: the segment geometry, stored as a line representation and used to draw
  the route on the map.
  \item \textbf{Street}: the street where the segment is located.
  \item \textbf{From Street}: the starting cross street or intersection reference.
  \item \textbf{To Street}: the ending cross street or intersection reference.
  \item \textbf{Facility Class}: the level of protection or treatment provided by the segment.
  This field is central to the visualization, because it distinguishes protected paths from lower-
  protection or shared facilities such as sharrow lanes.
  \item \textbf{Borough}: the borough in which the segment lies.
\end{itemize}

In the analysis, this structure is used to derive three families of summaries: counts by borough,
counts by status, and counts by facility class. These summaries help reveal how the network is
distributed across the city, which parts of the network are current, and which kinds of facilities are
most common. They also provide the rationale for how the map layer should be read and styled.

The raw file also contains a few columns that are not necessary for the analysis presented in this
report. The fields \texttt{prevbikeid}, \texttt{gwsys2}, \texttt{spur}, \texttt{ft2facilit}, and
\texttt{tf2facilit} were dropped during preprocessing because they do not contribute to the network
breakdowns or to the map rendering used in the final visualization.

This dataset is the spatial counterpart to the trip data. If the trip dataset explains demand, the
bike-lanes dataset explains supply: where cycling infrastructure exists, what level of protection it
offers, and how the network has changed over time. In the report, the exploration is organised around
three questions that directly connect to the final map.

The first question is where the network is concentrated. A borough-level breakdown shows that the
largest number of segments are in Manhattan and Brooklyn, while the outer boroughs contain fewer
segments. This does not merely describe a count; it reveals the geography of cycling investment. Dense
segment counts in central boroughs imply a more mature and interconnected infrastructure, while lower
counts elsewhere suggest a sparser network or a more fragmented build-out. The borough view therefore
helps explain why the final map appears most visually dense in the core of the city.

The second question is what kind of facilities dominate the network. The facility-class distribution is
important because it encodes the degree of separation between cyclists and traffic. Protected paths and
protected lanes support a very different riding experience from shared lanes or sharrows, and that
difference matters both analytically and visually. In the map, this field can be used to assign visual
priority, making the safer or more substantial infrastructure stand out. In the analysis, it also helps
us understand whether the city has expanded primarily through high-comfort infrastructure or through
lighter interventions that share space with motor vehicles.

The third question is how the network has grown over time. The installation timeline shows the build-up
of the system year by year and gives a sense of the pace of infrastructure expansion. Sudden increases in
the number of installed segments usually correspond to citywide policy pushes, corridor projects, or
larger protected-lane programmes. This timeline is useful for interpretation because it contextualises
the current network: a dense cluster of segments on the map may reflect decades of incremental growth,
or it may be the result of a recent acceleration in capital investment.

The map of the network is the key output of the dataset. By plotting \texttt{the\_geom}, the analysis
turns the table into a geographic layer that can be overlaid with trip demand and station data. The map
is not only descriptive but also diagnostic: it shows whether the dataset is spatially coherent, where
segments cluster, and how the network spans the city. Segments concentrate visually in Manhattan and
Brooklyn, reflecting the geographic distribution observed in the borough counts. In the final visualization,
this layer becomes the infrastructure context against which trip flows and station activity can be interpreted.

Taken together, these analyses show that the bike-lanes dataset is useful for more than just drawing a
line map. It supports a structural reading of the network, a historical reading of its expansion, and a
cartographic reading of its spatial coverage. These three views are complementary and together justify
why the dataset is included as a separate source in the project.

\subsection{Data Quality}
The bike-lanes data is comparatively clean for a public GIS-style dataset, but it still needs
preprocessing and careful interpretation.

One data-management issue is column redundancy. Several fields in the raw CSV are not relevant to the
analysis or to the final visualization. The removed columns \texttt{prevbikeid}, \texttt{gwsys2},
\texttt{spur}, \texttt{ft2facilit}, and \texttt{tf2facilit} do not change the core structure of the
segment table, so dropping them makes the dataset easier to work with without reducing analytical value.

Status handling is another important quality consideration. The raw file includes both current and
retired segments, and those records should not be mixed blindly. Retired segments are useful for
understanding how the network evolved, but they should be excluded or clearly separated when the goal is
to depict the active bike network. Otherwise, the map would overstate the amount of usable
infrastructure.

Date fields are also uneven. Installation and retirement dates may be missing for some records, and this
especially affects historical segments. As a result, the installation timeline should be read as a
slightly conservative view of growth: years with missing dates are undercounted, and the earliest phases
of the network may appear smaller than they really were.

The geometry field deserves special attention as well. Because \texttt{the\_geom} stores the route as a
line geometry, malformed or missing values prevent a segment from being rendered. Those rows are not
useful for the map layer and must be filtered or repaired before plotting. In practice, this is the main
reason the analysis uses a sampled set of segments for the interactive map: rendering the full dataset at once would be heavier and less responsive, while a sample is sufficient to demonstrate the overall
spatial pattern.
